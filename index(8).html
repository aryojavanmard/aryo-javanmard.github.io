AlphaBattle, work in progress

Teaching a small language model to win Pokémon battles

I'm post-training a 1.7B open model on my own 12 GB gaming GPU to play Pokémon Showdown Random Battles: first by imitating high-rated players, then by reinforcement learning on live battles. Success means win rate and ladder Elo, not convincing-sounding reasoning.

Battle Monitor

Every turn, the battle is written out as compact text (about 475 tokens). The model answers with a single token from a fixed set of 13: 1–4 for moves, A–D for Terastallize plus a move, and 5–9 for switches. Illegal tokens are masked out before choosing, so it can never make an illegal move.

Choosing the model

Why 1.7 billion parameters, not 12

A 12B model will run on a 12 GB card in 4-bit, but training needs room for gradients, optimiser state and activations too. On Windows the desktop also takes 1.5–2 GB, and going over the limit doesn't crash: it silently spills into system RAM and runs 10–30× slower.

In reinforcement learning, battles per hour matter more than parameter count. The strongest Pokémon networks in the literature are tiny specialists. A 1.7B model that plays tens of thousands of practice battles should beat an 8B model that only manages a few hundred.

Results so far

First live results: a 0.6B smoke test

Before spending hours on the real model, I distilled a heuristic bot into Qwen3-0.6B-Base (500 recorded battles, one epoch) and played it live on a local Showdown server, 40 games against each opponent.

Prior work

Pokémon battling is an active research area, so this builds on a lot of existing work rather than starting from scratch.

Pokémon Showdown and poke-env

The open-source battle simulator, run locally, and the Python library AlphaBattle uses to read battle state and send moves.

PokéLLMon (2024)

A prompted GPT-4 agent. It found LLMs tend to panic-switch and misremember mechanics, and that feeding them structured move information helps.

PokéChamp (2025)

Combines an LLM with minimax search. Much of its strength comes from the search, not the language model.

Metamon (UT Austin, 2025)

Small non-LLM transformers trained on human replays plus self-play. A key lesson I borrowed: pure self-play overfits, and a diverse opponent pool helps.

Foul Play

A strong search-based bot. I plan to use it as the expert ceiling to measure against.

The open question

Can a small, pretrained language model learn to play well with far fewer battles than a network trained from scratch, because it already knows the game from the web?

Risks, written down early

What I expect to be hard, written down before the results come in.

!

Specialist bots will probably win

Search bots and small RL networks play many more games per hour. The interesting result is how close a language model gets, and how few battles it needs.

!

Bot win rates can flatter

Beating heuristic bots doesn't mean beating humans. Only the public ladder settles that, so it's the final gate.

!

Replays hide information

Public replays only show what each player revealed, while a real player sees their whole team. Reconstructing the player's view without leaking the future is fiddly.

!

Random Battles are noisy

Team luck decides a lot of games, so every result is reported with confidence intervals and enough games to mean something.
